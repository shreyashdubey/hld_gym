New York City, 1990

One switch fixes itself.

At approximately 2:30 p.m. EST on Monday, January 15, a 4ESS toll switch in New York hit a minor hardware fault and did what it was built to do: stopped taking calls, ran recovery, told its neighbours to hold off. Four to six seconds. Routine, and normally invisible.

Then it came back. Its first call attempt told a neighbour New York was up. While that neighbour rewrote its status map, two more landed within an interval of 1/100th of a second. Data damaged. That processor took itself out of service; its mate took over, caught the next pair, died too. Then that neighbour's return did it to everyone connected to it. Larry Seese, AT&T's director of technology development, called it "a chain reaction".

Nothing held it, because a mid-December software update had put the flaw into all 114 4ESS switches at once. Recovery was the payload: a switch coming back healthy is what killed its neighbours, so every instinct — restart it, restore it faster — fed the loop.

What worked was subtraction. Suspend signalling on the backup links; starve the cascade of triggers. The last link cleared at 11:30 p.m. EST. On Tuesday the fleet went back to the previous program.

The update had been "rigorously tested in laboratory environments". Afterwards that laboratory reproduced the problem. Nobody had tested the coming back.

Your fleet runs one release everywhere. Name the failure that reaches every copy simultaneously. Write it down first.
