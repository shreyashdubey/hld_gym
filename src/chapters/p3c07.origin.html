Iris Associates, 1989

Two people, one document, and a phone line between the copies.

Lotus wrote Ray Ozzie's startup a $1.2 million check in ’84 and took no equity, only an option on software that did not exist. Six months in, the store was not the hard part. They "needed to take the concept of synchronization and replication much more seriously", Ozzie recalled in a 2020 Computer History Museum interview, so they hired Steve Beckhardt for it.

No internet to lean on. Notes servers dialled each other and traded documents store and forward, so replicas drifted between calls. Notes’ first sale came on December 7th of ’89.

Notes does not guess, and the Domino help still spells out the rule. Every document carries a $Revisions field, "which tracks the date and time of each document editing session", so a save can be caught sitting on a base the other replica has left. Both copies are kept. "The document edited and saved the most times becomes the main document" — the loser survives untouched, as a response document titled "Replication or Save Conflict".

The same help documents a merge option and its ceiling: only "if no fields conflict". Notes never loses the work. It files it one level down, under the winner, and waits for a human to open it.

Design Dropbox, says the interviewer. Two devices edit one file offline, neither edit may be lost. Where does the losing copy go, and who finds it? Write it down first.
