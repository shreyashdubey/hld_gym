Adelaide, 1858

Ownership was something you proved by reading.

Two buyers could each hire a conveyancer, trace the same chain of deeds, find the same seller entitled, and pay. Both searches were right. Search and payment were separate acts; the loser's remedy was a lawsuit.

Robert Torrens, the colony's former collector of customs, put it through as a private member's bill. The Real Property Act, assented 27th January, 1858: “No instrument shall be effectual to pass any estate…” — not until the Registrar-General enters it in the register book. The deed stopped moving land. The entry moved it. The Act wrote the tie-break: two instruments for the same land presented “at the same time”, and he registers whichever holder hands him the grant or certificate of title.

Without the signed parcel map he could require, “it shall not be incumbent on the Registrar-General to proceed…”. You cannot claim what the register cannot name.

An authoritative register can be authoritatively wrong. A court could cancel a fraudulent entry, but not one an innocent buyer had paid for. Past that, the remedy is money, priced before the register opened: “one farthing in the pound sterling” of land value into an Assurance Fund. Still its purpose: “Compensation is payable when a person is deprived of land…”. It pays the owner the register got wrong. It does not give the land back.

Two matchers pick driver Dev a millisecond apart — one for Asha, one for Ben. Name the one place that owns Dev, and what the loser does.
