Massachusetts General Hospital, 2010

On a January morning in 2010, an 89-year-old man's heart rate began to fall. His cardiac monitor worked. It raised the lower-priority alarms, beeped at the central nurses' station, scrolled the warning across three hallway signs. Ten nurses were on duty. None could recall hearing or seeing any of it. His heart slowed for 20 minutes and stopped. The one signal built to cut through — the crisis alarm on his monitor — had been switched off. Investigators never said why.

What they did record: nurses said they were desensitised, hearing alarms constantly all day. Alarm signals on a ward can reach several hundred per patient per day, and an estimated 85 to 99 percent of them need no clinical intervention. One 15-bed unit averaged 942 alarms a day — a critical one every 90 seconds.

Both failures are ones you can build. Switching off the crisis alarm is a per-recipient preference applied to a P0. The advisory flood is an unrate-limited bulk lane sharing its channel. The family settled for $850,000. The Joint Commission's Sentinel Event Alert 50 counted 98 alarm events, 80 of them deaths, and the contributing factor reported most often was alarms inappropriately turned off.

No threshold fixes this. Tighten one and you silence a real event you cannot name in advance.

Which gates should a P0 skip, and which must it still pass? Write it before you read on.
