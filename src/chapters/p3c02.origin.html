USPTO, 1995

No network, no disk, one keypress of budget.

Dale Grover, Martin King and Clifford Kushler are making a nine-key pad type English. Press 2-2-7-3 and you have asked for a word, not a spelling. Something must rank the candidates, and the device cannot: no server, no memory to spare, the answer due before your thumb lifts.

So the ranking is done in advance and ships as storage order. Their patent, granted 6 October 1998, is blunt: "Within the memory, the words are stored in order of decreasing frequency of use so that the proper order is automatically presented." Nothing ranks at keypress time. Even the lookup keys are discarded and regenerated mid-search, memory being "a critical factor for mobile phones".

Frozen is the feature. In the preferred embodiment the base never moves, "so that the sequence of possible words presented to the user is always the same". Whatever you meant goes in a small private vocabulary bolted on the side.

Then the bill. Across 9,025 sample words, 8,437 (95%) resolved uniquely. You settle the rest by thumb. The patent passed to Tegic in 1998, Tegic to AOL: its fiscal 2000 annual report prices Tegic at "approximately 4.8 million shares of common stock". And 2-2-7-3 still gives you "case" — then care, base, card, bare, cape, five presses to reach a word the corpus ranked below the rest years ago.

Your ranking is frozen at build time and the user wanted something else. Where must that correction live? Write it before you read on.
