Three Mile Island, 1979

A wall of instruments telling four men the wrong thing.

At 4:00 a.m. on March 28, 1979, feedwater to TMI-2 stopped. A relief valve on the pressurizer opened as designed. Thirteen seconds into the accident it was commanded shut. It didn't.

The operators had a lamp for that valve, wired to the solenoid that carried the command, not to the valve stem. The President's Commission: the light "showed only that the signal had been sent to close the PORV rather than the fact that the PORV remained open."

The panel read shut while the coolant escaped: 32,000 gallons, over a third of the coolant, in 100 minutes. The valve stayed open 2 hours and 22 minutes.

More signal did not save them. More than 100 alarms lit in the first minutes, with "no system for suppressing the unimportant signals." The alarm printer ran behind the events, then jammed. Operator Craig Faust told the Commission: "I would have liked to have thrown away the alarm panel. It wasn't giving us any useful information."

Kemeny priced it at $1 to $2 billion and found the control room "seriously deficient under accident conditions." Cleanup ran another fourteen years.

The worst finding is the quiet one. A shift supervisor testified that "there had never been less than 52 alarms lit in the control room" — on an ordinary day.

Which of your alerts measures a command you sent, not an effect a user got? Name it before you read on.
