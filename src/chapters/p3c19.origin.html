IBM, New York, 1949

A priest with no money asks for thirty years of machine time.

Roberto Busa wanted every word Thomas Aquinas wrote — in, et, non included — findable by where it occurred. For his doctorate he had written 10,000 cards by hand for one preposition. The corpus ran to more than ten million words.

Busa wrote in 1980 that Thomas J. Watson Sr. had on his desk "a report which said that IBM machines could never do what I wanted". Busa carried the waiting-room poster in with him: "The difficult we do right away; the impossible takes a little longer." Watson agreed, "provided that you do not change IBM into International Busa Machines." Busa could pay nothing.

They built an inverted index in cardboard. One word on each card, carrying that word's reference — its place in the text. Sort it alphabetically and every card for a term stands together: term, then the list of places. That sort returned 130,000 distinct written forms.

Then the part no machine touched. Amabat and amare are one term only because a human says so. Ten priests spent two full years building the table that decided it, and the texts were read by eye at least nine times.

Computer hours came to fewer than 10,000. Man hours came to more than a million.

Five hundred million listings, five thousand edits a second. Of Busa's three costs — build, sort, decide what a term is — which bites first? Write it before you read on.
