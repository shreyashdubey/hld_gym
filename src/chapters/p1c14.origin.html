Cambridge, Massachusetts, 1999

Every ID scheme buys uniqueness from somewhere. Paul Leach and Rich Salz wrote down where UUIDs bought theirs in February 1998: "no centralized authority is required to administer them (beyond the one that allocates IEEE 802.1 node identifiers)". Read the parenthesis twice. A version-1 UUID is a timestamp, a clock sequence, and 48 bits that are the machine's Ethernet MAC address. Coordination was not skipped. It was borrowed, and shipped inside every ID.

Office 97 stamped one into every document you saved. Richard Smith, 45, president of Phar Lap Software, had said so publicly that month.

Melissa arrived Friday, March 26, 1999, and by Monday sat on more than 100,000 computers at more than 300 organizations. Smith opened the infected document. "I started looking for files which contained the same Ethernet address", he said. By Sunday he had matched it to older files by a writer calling himself VicodinES, and gave the FBI a lead. David L. Smith later admitted more than $80 million in damage, and drew 20 months and a $5,000 fine.

Microsoft shipped the Office 97 Unique Identifier Patch. The bill stands. Strip the MAC out and you have UUIDv4: nothing borrowed, nothing leaked, and 122 random bits landing anywhere in your B-tree. Put the timestamp back in front, as UUIDv7 does, and writes land in order again while every ID tells its holder when it was made.

Your order IDs go out in URLs tomorrow. Write down what each scheme leaks.
