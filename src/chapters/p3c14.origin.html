Wall Street, 1985

On Wednesday, 20 November 1985, trades in more than 32,000 government securities issues poured into the Bank of New York, which cleared Treasuries for the Street. Its software was specified for 36,000. Short of that it broke, and “unknown to anyone, the computer began to corrupt the transactions”. The database of accounts and transactions was damaged.

Next morning it could not tell the Fed where to forward the bonds arriving for its customers. They kept arriving. Fedwire settles delivery-versus-payment, so every delivery automatically debited the bank's reserve account to pay the seller. The effect committed. The record did not.

By evening the overdraft was $32 billion. The bank's total assets were $15.9 billion. A patch, which the chairman described as the electronic equivalent of patching a tire, failed just after midnight. At 2:15 a.m. the Fed's credit totalled $23.6 billion overnight, secured by every asset it had and every customer security it could pledge. Interest: about $5 million, near 7 percent of nine months' earnings.

None of it was missing. It was unrecorded. They rebuilt Wednesday's trades by 5 a.m., Thursday's by 10; by then Friday's deliveries were stacking in the same account. At about 11:30 a.m. Corrigan stopped the wire into the bank, because nothing else could stop the record falling further behind the money.

You are about to design a payment system. Before you read on, write down what must be true of the record at the instant the money moves—and who checks it.
