Amsterdam, 2005

Wikimedia's answer to Europe was a data centre with nothing in it that could think.

Wikipedia's servers sat in Tampa. Wikimedia's 2005 notes clock a French node at 110 ms to Florida, 4-5 ms to Squids near Paris. The Amsterdam site, started around 2005, did the same at scale: a "caching-only" cluster, no application servers.

A cache abroad only pays if the page inside it is the page everyone wants. MediaWiki was written to make sure it was. From its own source: "maintain different caches for logged-in users and non-logged in ones". A page reaches Squid only when no session cookie is present, so an anonymous article is byte-identical for everyone. Nothing renders it per person, because nothing is allowed to.

Expiry was rejected outright — only the newest revision may ever be served — so an edit multicasts an HTCP message ordering every Squid to "completely forget about an entity". Wikimedia's architecture deck reports: hit rates of 85% for Text, 98% for Media, 30 000 HTTP requests/s during peak-time, 350 servers "managed by ~ 6 people", on a yearly budget of around $2M.

Then log in. The cookie appears, the edge stands down, and every page is built for you. The cache had become the budget, and the budget started editing the product: "If a MediaWiki feature is too expensive, it doesn't get enabled on Wikipedia."

Before you read on: what would you change about your pages — not your cache — so an edge could serve them?
