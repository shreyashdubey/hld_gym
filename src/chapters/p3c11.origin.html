RBS Group, June 2012

A bank's day ends when the overnight batch finishes, not when the branches close.

The batch applies the day's withdrawals, deposits, clearing and standing orders "in the correct sequence", and is done only when every balance is final. On Sunday 17 June Technology Services, the RBS Group's central IT function, upgraded the scheduler behind NatWest and Ulster Bank from Version 1 to Version 2A. Monday night's run limped. Tuesday they rolled it back — and nobody had tested rolling back 2A. They had tested rolling back 2.

Jobs stopped appearing: "a significant number of jobs failed to appear in the batch queues and the unprocessed batch jobs began to multiply."

There was no replay button. Staff re-loaded jobs by hand, in order, because out of order gives wrong balances and wrong interest. By 21 June Ulster Bank was over a day behind, so the next night's batch began before the current one finished, colliding in the same queues.

6.5 million customers. Missed mortgages, unmet payrolls, cards declined abroad. £70.3 million in redress, £42 million from the FCA, £14 million from the PRA. An internal audit had named the risk of a batch scheduler failure twenty-two months earlier.

Ulster Bank's scheduler returned to full functionality on 10 July. Nothing waited for them: every midnight stacked a fresh batch on the one they had not cleared.

You are the candidate: "design a job scheduler — cron, but distributed." Which failure would you name first? Write it before you read on.
