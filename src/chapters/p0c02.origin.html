Palo Alto, 2000

The fraud desk was the product.

PayPal was winning and being eaten. In the first nine months of 2000 it booked $5.6 million and provisioned $7.7 million against losses. One episode, July to October, cost $5.7 million in unauthorised charge-backs.

Max Levchin's team built a detector — "PayPal's proprietary IGOR system", named "after a Russian hacker it helped detect". Igor never ran alone. By 2001 a team of 100 employees, about one-sixth of the company, read Igor's graphics for anomalies, and "humans review the evidence and decide whether to freeze" the account.

That was not an engineering gap. The filing says why: "technically knowledgeable criminals will continue to attempt to circumvent our anti-fraud systems". A KDD paper later wrote the rule: "the performance of a classifier can degrade rapidly after it is deployed"; the remedy on offer was "repeated, manual, ad hoc reconstruction". The analyst cannot be shipped out.

Peter Thiel co-founded Palantir in 2003 and sold that arrangement itself: "forward deployed engineers", posted beside the user to learn "how and why they make decisions".

By 2020 it had not paid for itself. Losses every year since inception, $579.6 million in 2019, and engineers left parked on a customer "even if those allocations reduce contribution margin". Nobody has automated the part that finds the problem.

A company that cannot automate its analyst has to hire people who can sit beside one. Write down what its interview must test, before you read what four companies test.
