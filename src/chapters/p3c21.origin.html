American Fork, Utah, 2006

Move Networks sold video with no video server. It cut a file into what its patent calls "streamlets", each "between about 0.1 and 5 seconds", then encoded every streamlet at every bitrate. Not a stream. A grid. The December 2006 client showcase held three names.

Everything follows from who switches. The claim puts it at the far end: "each of the end user stations initiate each of the shifts between the different bit rates". If the player picks per chunk, the encoder cannot know which rung it will want, so it must build them all first. And the origin stops being a server: streamlets "may essentially be static files", free to be "cached by cache servers of Internet Service Providers". Ladder and CDN bill, one sentence.

ABC shipped full-length HD on 24 July 2007. The on-screen bitrate meter read 850 kbps — and the catch: Move "requires a small player applet to be downloaded to the computer".

Apple's Roger Pantos published the same architecture on 1 May 2009 as a text Playlist of URIs — a published format, not a download.

EchoStar bought Move's assets on the last day of 2010 for $45 million. The grid outlived the company. It still has to be built whole before anyone presses play, on a user-generated firehose where most videos never find one.

Most uploads are watched by almost nobody, yet every rung costs compute before the first play. Which rungs would you build up front? Write it down first.
