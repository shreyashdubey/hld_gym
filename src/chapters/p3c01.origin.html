London, 1967

W. H. Smith was computerising its warehouse, and computers cannot read title pages. The Publishers Association published nine pages by F. G. Foster, "Standard numbering in the book trade", 1967. The trade took up nine digits, the last a validation character; ISO widened it to ten.

Watch how it hands numbers out — you will build this again. No book gets its number from a central counter. An agency gives a publisher one registrant element, a block, and the publisher numbers its books out of it. Coordination happens once, at the block, never per book. The block's width is a forecast: it "varies in length according to the projected publication output of each publisher". Every digit spent on the block is a digit the books never get.

Then the space ran out. ISO: "the very success of the ISBN system, up to now based on 10 digits, has been causing it to run out of capacity". An assigned ISBN may never be reused, and no identifier widens in place under readers you do not control. The notice went out in 2006: everyone had until 1 January 2007, and the standard sold for 99 Swiss francs. Every catalogue and till on earth changed primary key that day, because ten digits had once looked like plenty.

Forty-five minutes: "design a URL shortener." Before you read on, write down why the code is seven characters — and what it costs the world when seven runs out.
