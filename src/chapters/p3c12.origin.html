AT&T and DIMACS, 2003

Two teams, one wall, opposite sides.

The network had to measure itself. Even sampled and aggregated, AT&T's backbone summaries ran to 500 Gbytes a day, about ten billion fifty-byte records. Cranor, Johnson and colleagues ran Gigascope on such streams: seven installations, the largest peaking at 1.2 million packets per second. At that rate nobody gets a counter per flow. You get a fixed array, one pass.

In June 2003, Graham Cormode and S. Muthukrishnan, the latter listed on the report at "Rutgers University and AT&T Research", filed a DIMACS tech report. They gave up exactness, then chose which way to be wrong: "The error bound here is one-sided, as opposed to all previous constructions which gave two-sided errors." Count into a few cells, read back the smallest. Collisions only add, so an answer is never below the truth.

The sign buys a sentence nobody else could write: "there is absolute certainty that this procedure will find and output every heavy hitter". An impostor can climb in; a real winner cannot fall out.

Overshoot is at most ε‖a‖₁, with probability 1−δ; at width w = ⌈e/ε⌉ that is this chapter's (e/w)×N. It scales with traffic crossed, not keys counted. Memory holds flat under a flood of junk keys. The error does not: a worm raises the floor under every flow you built the thing to watch.
