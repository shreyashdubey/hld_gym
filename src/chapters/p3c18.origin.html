Ohio · 14 August 2003

At 14:14 EDT the alarm process inside FirstEnergy's GE XA/21 energy management system stalled. Two processes had grabbed one data structure at once; GE's Mike Unum later said the corruption left "the alarm event application getting into an infinite loop and spinning". Nothing rang. Nothing printed. The thing that announces failure had failed, and it had no way to announce that.

Everything else kept running. That was the trap. The strip-chart printers kept scrolling — "the chart pens showed only the last valid measurement recorded, without any variation". A flat line. Steady, if you did not know.

At 14:41 the primary server died under the backlog. The stalled alarm application failed over intact onto the hot standby, which died 13 minutes later. "For over an hour no one in FE's control room grasped that their computer systems were not operating properly". Neighbouring utilities telephoned; the operators discounted them, because their screens disagreed.

Three lines sagged into trees, unseen. At 16:05:57 a 345-kV line tripped, and within seven minutes the cascade crossed eight states and Ontario: 61,800 MW gone, 50 million people dark, $4 to $10 billion in US costs.

The alarms never came back that afternoon. An alarm system can be set up to alarm operators when it itself fails. The task force checked, and wrote one sentence. "FE's EMS did not have such a notification system."

Your monitoring has gone quiet and everything looks fine. How would you tell those apart? Write it before you read on.
