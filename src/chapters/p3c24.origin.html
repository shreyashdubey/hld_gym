ETH Zurich, 1998

Roger Weber and Hans-J. Schek at ETH Zurich, with Stephen Blott at Bell Laboratories, already had their design. They had written it up as a technical report a year earlier. Then they measured the competition: the R*-tree, the X-tree, a decade of trees built for similarity search.

The measurement came back as a proof. Above around 10 dimensions, partitioning and clustering indexes are "outperformed on average by a simple sequential scan". On their real data — 50,000 images, 45-dimensional colour vectors — the trees lost "by orders of magnitude". Not a tuning problem. Any partitioning scheme "must degenerate to a sequential scan through all their blocks" as dimensions grow, because at that dimensionality almost every cell of the space is empty and nothing is left to prune.

So the thing they had was not an index. The VA-File keeps 4 to 8 bits per dimension and scans that instead, a file "4 to 8 times smaller than the vector file". Their paper's goal, stated plainly: make "the unavoidable sequential scan as fast as possible".

That is the whole prize. A constant factor on a scan nobody can avoid. Everything faster than that — every graph, cluster and quantizer in this chapter — buys its speed by returning the wrong answer sometimes, and all you get to choose is how often.

Your corpus is 5 million vectors, not a billion. What does that result say you should build? Write it before you read on.
