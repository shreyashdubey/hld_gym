Downey, California, 1968

North American Rockwell had “won the bid to launch the first spacecraft to the moon”. It needed one question answered all day: what goes into this assembly? In 1965 it asked IBM for a system to manage large bills of material. In 1966, 12 IBM people, 10 from Rockwell and 3 from Caterpillar Tractor started on ICS and DL/I.

A bill of materials is a tree, so they stored it as one. “Because data are always accessed hierarchically”, DL/I walks parent to child down the disk, and that walk costs nothing. ICS was installed in April 1968. On 14 August the first “IMS READY” reached an IBM 2740 terminal at the Rockwell Space Division.

The access path was not a query plan; it was the disk layout. Ask which assemblies use a given bolt and you ran the tree backwards: a new program, often a new database.

Codd, at IBM San Jose, named the fault in 1970 on the same industry: parts and projects. Five hierarchies hold the same facts; a program written for one “will fail on at least three of the remaining structures”. He called it “Access Path Dependence”.

In 1998 IBM's anniversary note still counted “over 90 percent of the top worldwide companies” running IMS daily. The access path had been chosen for a parts list, and nothing running on it could be stopped long enough to choose again.

Name a database you have used that sells the same trade, and what it forces you to decide first.
