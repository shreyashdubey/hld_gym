Berkeley, October 1986

Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory and UC Berkeley sat 400 yards and two IMP hops apart. Throughput between them fell from 32 Kbps to 40 bps. Nobody attacked anything. The network was doing its job: holding what it could not send yet. Van Jacobson and Michael Karels opened their 1988 paper with that “factor-of-thousand drop in bandwidth”.

John Nagle had already published the reason. RFC 970, December 1985, assumed the safest switch imaginable: infinite storage, one that cannot run out of buffers. Nagle proved it is the doomed one. Give packets a finite lifetime and that network “will, under overload, drop all packets”. The queue grows until the wait through it exceeds the packets' lifetime. What leaves the far end has one second left; the next hop decrements that to zero and discards it. Every packet was stored, forwarded and destroyed — long after its sender gave up and retransmitted.

Nagle had named the state in January 1984: “every packet is being transmitted several times and throughput is reduced to a small fraction of normal”. It is stable.

There was a message for slow down. It proved “an ineffective (and unfair) antidote for congestion”, and RFC 6633 told transports to ignore it in May 2012. The only way a network can say stop is to destroy your packet — a signal you cannot tell apart from a broken wire.

You are the loaded router. You cannot send “slow down” either. Write what you do with the next request.
