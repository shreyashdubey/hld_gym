Dalmellington, 2015

The books balanced. The books were wrong.

A subpostmistress scanned a pouch of £8,000 out of her core branch into her outreach branch. Once. Horizon booked it four times.

Her log-on script had not closed cleanly, so the pouch delivery script "thought it had not finished and attempted to repeat the last part of the script". Each repeat wrote a fresh remittance. Her outreach receipts came to £32,000 against eight thousand pounds of cash, leaving her £24,000 short.

Nothing objected. Each repeat was recorded like any other remittance; the accounts agreed with themselves all the way down. Fujitsu's architect told the High Court the bug "had the effect of making it look as though a user was simply doing something multiple times". Same bug, 88 branches.

Ten years earlier at Callendar Square it ran backwards: writes stalled, transactions vanished off the counter, staff re-keyed them, transfers doubled. Fujitsu's support note said the opposite: missing-looking transactions "should not be re-entered".

She could not check. Subpostmasters saw their own transaction log for 60 days, on a paper till roll. Fujitsu could inject entries into branch accounts unasked, and it "would look as though the SPM had done it".

Roughly 1,000 people were prosecuted and convicted on Horizon data. Double-entry proves only that a ledger agrees with itself. It cannot count the cash in the drawer, and the woman holding the drawer could not read the ledger.

Every entry balanced and the retry was honest. What must the receiver remember, and where?
