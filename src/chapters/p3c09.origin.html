Nexor, 1994

Politeness was never designed. It was conceded.

In 2019 Martijn Koster dug out the report his server mailed him in September 1993: the same sequence of pages pulled three times over, "in the order of 1 document per second".

He asked for no protocol change. On 25 February 1994 he told the www-talk list that "Robots are one of the few aspects of the web that cause operational problems and cause people grief", and proposed a text file robots could read if they felt like it.

Charles Stross pointed Websnarf, his Perl spider, at a website about spiders. Koster's. The only emails Koster could find are dated ten days later. Stross recalled in 2009 he had "unintentionally invented the denial of service attack". Koster asked him to stop. He stopped. That was the enforcement layer.

Consensus landed 30 June 1994. Its status section: "It is not enforced by anybody." Two fields, User-agent and Disallow; anything unrecognised, ignored.

The IETF ratified it as RFC 9309, Standards Track, September 2022. It gained an Allow line and a 24-hour cache rule. It still cannot state a rate. "These rules are not a form of access authorization."

A server can say where not to go. It cannot say how often, and cannot make you listen. Whatever delay you honour, you compute it and carry it, and nobody on the far end will ever check.

Nothing outside your crawler enforces your politeness. Where must the per-host delay live, then? Write it before you read on.
