BBN Labs, February 1988

Staff at the CSNET Coordination and Information Center kept fielding one question: why is a single mail message delivered several times? Craig Partridge traced it: no mailer was broken, the hole was in SMTP.

A sender ends the message with a single dot; the receiver replies 250 — accepted. Partridge's February 1988 memo names what sits between: "the message is active at both the sending and receiving mailer." The sender must assume it was not delivered, so it resends. The receiver must assume it was, so it delivers. Break the link there and both copies go out.

The gap should be milliseconds. It ran minutes: many receivers answered only after expanding a mailing list or writing the mailboxes. Partridge put it second only to mail loops among causes of duplicate mail on the Internet.

Half his fix is this chapter's rule: reply as soon as the message is "safely put in a non-volatile (e.g., disk) queue." Persist, then ack. The other half he never wrote. His whole remedy is to shrink the gap, not to close it: ack sooner, and let senders wait longer — ten minutes, he suggested, would not be unreasonable. RFC 1123, October 1989, made exactly that the floor. RFC 5321 (2008) still repeats his sentence and cites him. No dedup rule was ever added, and mail still arrives twice.

Your chat server has the same gap. What must be true before the single tick — and what must the phone send so a retry is not a second message?
