Cologne, 1974

A currency trade is two writes to two systems, and nobody owns both.

On 26 June 1974, at 15:30 CET, the German authorities closed Bankhaus Herstatt — after Germany's interbank payment system had shut for the day, while New York was still open. Counterparties had already paid Deutsche marks in Frankfurt against dollars due later the same day. Herstatt's New York correspondent suspended dollar payments from its account. The second leg never left.

Everyone who had paid was exposed for the full value sent. The BIS put the transactions awaiting settlement at $200 million. Then it spread: New York banks stopped paying anyone until their own countervalue arrived, and over three days gross funds through the city's netting system fell an estimated 60%.

The BIS wrote the missing property down in 1996 — one currency moves "if and only if" the other does. Nobody owned that guarantee, so somebody had to build it. CLS Bank went live in September 2002 across seven currencies: a coordinator with a pay-in schedule, hourly funding deadlines, and a settlement window of 07:00 to 12:00 CET — overlapping the operating hours of six of its seven national payment systems.

That is what atomicity across two systems costs: a five-hour window everyone stands inside. CLS covers 18 currencies, and $2.2 trillion of daily turnover in April 2022 still settled without that guarantee.

Your order service and your payment service are Frankfurt and New York. Write down what you would build to make them one trade.
