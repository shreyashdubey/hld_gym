Tohoku coast, 2011

P-waves arrive first and do little harm. S-waves arrive later and bring the ceiling down. The gap is the entire warning.

Seismic arrivals reach stations in distance order — nearest sensor first, the rest trickling in behind it. Every extra station you wait for sharpens the magnitude and shortens the warning, so the Japan Meteorological Agency does not wait for completeness. It declares a cutoff. On 11 March 2011 it issued the first early warning 5.4 s after the first P-wave detection, ahead of the S-wave everywhere in Japan.

Then the aftershocks overlapped, and every late arrival carried a second question: which earthquake is this from? The system answered from travel times, and it answered wrong. Some of the near source stations of the second event were treated as far source stations of the first — real reading, wrong window — so a modest quake was sized as a monster. In the two months after the main shock, 44 inappropriate warnings were issued among the 70 broadcast to the public. Over 70 per cent of them came from concurrent events.

No cutoff is right for two overlapping earthquakes. Wait long enough to tell them apart and the warning is worthless. JMA's replacement for the association step, IPF, reached production on 14 December 2016. In January 2018 two quakes about 400 km apart broke together, and the M4.5 was sized from the M4.0's displacement amplitudes.
