LiveJournal, 2003

The databases were the emergency. The cure was for sale.

The fix was 64-bit database machines with more memory. Brad Fitzpatrick would not buy them: "recall that I'm stubborn and frugal." What he had free was idle RAM across 40+ Web nodes, and, "sick of how painful it is to cache efficiently in mod_perl applications", he spent that instead.

On 27 May 2003 he committed 1,195 lines of C under the message "committing memcached". Its README states the design in one clause: "it should never go to disk, or you've lost the whole point of it."

Every absence follows: no durability, no replication, no auth. Three days later Anatoly Vorobey swapped in power-of-two slab classes and "always allocate slabs of 1MB" — the page your cache still fights over. And no membership: "Each Memcached instance is totally independent, and does not communicate with the others." Nothing knows the cluster, so the client hashes the key % num_buckets.

By November 2004 that was LiveJournal's whole cache: 28 instances on ten hosts, none dedicated, holding 30 GB at a 90-93% hit rate, ~100,000 queries/second at peaks. It cost no new machines.

And num_buckets could not change. His own 2004 to-do slide already asked to "grow/shrink memcached farm at run-time" — adding one box rehashes nearly every key, and the miss storm lands on the databases the cache exists to spare.

You build the cache now — no Redis, no Memcached. Which 2003 deletion would you keep, and which would you pay to undo?
