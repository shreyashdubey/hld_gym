Murray Hill, 1982

The write-up, that year, of work already shipped.

Doug McIlroy’s problem at Bell Labs was arithmetic. Unix spell’s word list ran to about 250,000 bytes of clear text. A PDP-11 could address 32,768 sixteen-bit words in total.

So he stopped storing words. Dennis Ritchie supplied “a simple superimposed code scheme first proposed by Bloom”: 25,000 words across 400,000 bits, 11 hash functions, remainders modulo the eleven largest primes below 400,000. The words were gone: ask whether a string is a word, never which.

He checked which way the error runs. A false hit lets a misspelling through; it can never flag a word spelled right. Drafts rarely hold more than twenty bad words, so at one false acceptance in 2,000, “a mistake will be accepted in about 1 in 100 documents”. spell may miss. It may not contradict the writer.

Then the list grew. “Experience showed, however, that 25,000 words wasn’t enough”. Thirty thousand wanted a bigger table; there was none. You cannot delete from a filter, cannot resize it in place, cannot buy back an error rate the fill has spent. The rate is words over bits; only one was his to move.

So the filter went too, replaced by exact hash values stored as differences: 13.60 bits a word against a 13.57-bit floor. The sketch was never the destination. It was the part that ran out first.

Your filter is filling the same way. Write what you watch, and what you do at the threshold.
