Abermule, Wales, 1921

A lock that did not fail.

The single line to Newtown ran on paired electric instruments. Pull a tablet out of one and the circuit breaks: no second tablet comes out until the first goes back. One tablet, one section. It had worked that way since 1890; the risk was reckoned "so remote as to be negligible".

26 January 1921. A porter released the Newtown tablet for the up express and told nobody. The down train pulled in. A booking clerk of fifteen took the tablet it had just surrendered — Montgomery–Abermule, the section behind it — and gave it to relief-stationmaster Frank Lewis, who assumed it had been exchanged. Lewis passed the pouch to the fireman "without looking what tablet it contained". The driver was oiling his engine. Both tablets were numbered No. 18; only the raised letters round the rim named the section.

Two trains, one section. They met head-on at about 12.6 p.m. Seventeen died, thirty-six were injured. Four employees were dismissed.

Pringle's inquiry did not fault the instrument. It had been right all morning, and Newtown had issued the correct tablet. What he faulted was slipshod handling "combined with failure on the part of enginemen to examine tablets". Holding the token only tells you what was true when someone handed it to you.

The instrument was correct. The grant was correct. Write down where the check has to move, and who makes it.
